When Training Labels Change Who Gets Screened

Temporal evaluation of diagnosis-based versus biomarker-augmented labels in CDC NHANES under finite screening capacity

Cao Thien An Nguyen
Department of Economics & Data Science
University of Wisconsin–Madison
Independent Research Study • 2026

01 The Clinical Problem

Population screening triage algorithms frequently train on administrative proxy labels (e.g. billing codes, prior clinical diagnoses) because objective laboratory tests are resource-constrained.

However, clinical diagnoses require clinical encounters. When screening algorithms learn from diagnosis history, individuals facing healthcare access barriers are misclassified as healthy baselines, distorting screening queues.

02 Study Design & Temporal Protocol

We evaluated whether replacing administrative labels with biomarker-augmented labels changes triage selection, holding non-invasive features and screening capacity constant.

Feature Space Constraint: Both models use identical non-invasive features (Age, Sex, BMI, Waist circumference, Poverty Income Ratio). Lab tests and healthcare encounters were strictly barred from model inputs.

03 Data Engineering & Quality Note

Embedded Lakehouse: Ingested 10 SAS transport (.XPT) cycles into an in-process DuckDB analytical lakehouse, executing SQL-driven cohort exclusions and exporting zero-copy Parquet files.

Data Contracts: Pandera schemas verified biological bounds (HbA1c: 2.0% to 25.0%), confirmed 0 pregnant participants, and logged stable covariate distributions across survey cycles (PSI < 0.04 for age, BMI, waist).

Key Finding
Changing training labels altered screening priorities and identified two additional potential cases at the same screening capacity.

04 Paired-Selection Queue Analysis at 20% Clinic Capacity Capacity K = 939 slots / 4,698 adults

At 20% fixed screening capacity, both models select exactly 939 individuals. However, the queues diverge: 878 participants are shared, while each model exclusively selects 61 individuals.

Important Scientific Scope Note: The 3.0× higher precision (4.92% vs 1.64%) applies strictly to the 61-person swapped boundary subgroup, not to overall model precision (which is 5.86% vs 5.64% unweighted). Overall case yield increased from 53 to 55 (+2 potential cases).

Small Denominator Notice: The uninsured evaluation cohort contains only 13 latent cases in total. Model B identifies 1 additional case (from 2/13 to 3/13). While directionally encouraging, this small sample cannot establish definitive algorithmic fairness or disparity elimination.

06 Multi-Architecture Robustness

To evaluate model dependency, we re-evaluated triage under alternative decision tree algorithms:

  • Random Forest: Model A detects 60 cases; Model B detects 61 cases (+1 case, 77 swaps).
  • HistGradientBoosting: Model A detects 59 cases; Model B detects 59 cases (0 net gain, 132 swaps).

Confirms that priority shifts occur across architectures, while total yield gains remain modest (+0 to +2 cases).

07 Limitations & Scope

  • Surveillance vs Diagnosis: Single venous HbA1c ≥ 6.5% represents an elevated screening criterion, not an autonomous clinical diabetes diagnosis.
  • Survey Uncertainty: Complex survey confidence intervals overlap between models at all budget thresholds.
  • Operational Assumptions: Model simulations assume 100% testing uptake among prioritized individuals and uniform test costs.
